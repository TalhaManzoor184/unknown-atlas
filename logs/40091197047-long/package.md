## Voiceover script

**[HOOK]** In February 1959, nine experienced hikers cut their own tent open from the inside and fled into sub-zero darkness. None of them survived. For decades, this tragedy in the northern Ural Mountains has baffled investigators, spawning wild theories of UFOs, secret military weapons, and Yeti attacks. But what does the actual evidence tell us about those final hours?
  _Pronunciation: Dyatlov = DYAHT-lov_

**[INTRO]** The story began in January 1959. A group of ten students and graduates from the Ural Polytechnical Institute set out on a challenging trek across the Ural Mountains. Their destination was Gora Otorten, a peak known for harsh, unpredictable weather. Led by twenty-three-year-old Igor Dyatlov, the group was seasoned and well-prepared for winter expedition conditions.
  _Pronunciation: Otorten = oh-tor-TEN_

**[CONTEXT]** On January 28, group member Yuri Yudin had to turn back due to sudden joint pain and a heart condition. His departure left nine hikers to continue alone. Days later, on January 31, the group cached surplus equipment and started through the pass. Caught in deteriorating weather and low visibility, they accidentally deviated from their route toward the slopes of Kholat Syakhl, a mountain whose indigenous Mansi name translates chillingly to Dead Mountain.
  _Pronunciation: Kholat Syakhl = kho-LAT syakh-L_

**[CONTEXT]** When the group failed to return by mid-February, a search party was launched. On February 26, searchers made a grim discovery on the slope of Kholat Syakhl. The hikers' tent was found half-torn down, heavily weighted with snow. It had been sliced open from the inside with knives. All their boots, warm clothing, and survival gear were left behind, abandoned in freezing temperatures that dropped to minus forty degrees Celsius.

**[EVIDENCE]** Investigators traced nine sets of footprints leading away from the tent. Made by socks, a single shoe, or bare feet, the tracks headed down toward the edge of a nearby forest one point five kilometers away. After about five hundred meters, the footprints became covered in snow, leaving searchers to follow the trail blindly into the timberline.

**[EVIDENCE]** The first bodies were found beneath a large Siberian pine tree near the remains of a small fire. Georgiy Krivonishenko and Yuri Doroshenko were shoeless and dressed only in their underwear. Noticeably, branches on the tree were broken up to five meters high, suggesting someone had climbed it to look for something in the distance.

**[EVIDENCE]** Between the pine tree and the campsite, searchers found three more bodies. Igor Dyatlov, Zinaida Kolmogorova, and Rustem Slobodin were discovered at varying distances, in positions that suggested they were desperately trying to crawl back up the slope toward the safety of the tent.

**[EVIDENCE]** The remaining four hikers were missing for months. It wasn't until May, when the spring snow melted, that searchers discovered Lyudmila Dubinina, Semyon Zolotaryov, Nicolas Thibeaux-Brignolles, and Alexander Kolevatov buried under four meters of snow in a ravine deeper in the woods. Remarkably, some of these bodies wore clothing scavenged from their companions who had already died.

**[EVIDENCE]** While six of the hikers died of hypothermia, forensic examinations revealed horrifying injuries on the others. Thibeaux-Brignolles suffered major skull damage. Dubinina and Zolotaryov had massive chest fractures. Medical experts noted that inflicting such severe internal trauma required a mechanical force comparable to a severe car crash, yet there were no external soft tissue wounds matching those blows.

**[EVIDENCE]** Adding to the horror, the four bodies found in the creek bed in May were missing their eyes, tongues, and facial tissue. For years, conspiracy theorists pointed to these mutilations as proof of foul play or secret government experiments. However, the lead forensic expert, Vladimir Vozrozhdenny, established that this missing soft tissue was caused post-mortem by running stream water over the course of several weeks.

**[TWIST]** The lack of clear answers in nineteen fifty-nine opened the door to decades of speculation. Some theories suggested a clash with indigenous Mansi people, though police investigations found no signs of a struggle. Others blamed military rocket tests, infrasound-induced panic, or even ball lightning. Popular culture eventually embraced even wilder ideas, inspiring fictional films about secret teleportation experiments.

**[TWIST]** Observers at the hikers' funerals noted another strange detail. Friends and relatives recalled that the victims' skin possessed a deep brown tan or orange hue. Modern medical analysis notes this discoloration can occur in individuals who succumb to extreme cold, adding another piece to the physiological puzzle of the disaster.

**[CONCLUSION]** In recent years, authorities and scientists revisited the case. Russian officials officially reopened the investigation in twenty-nineteen, concluding in twenty-twenty that an avalanche forced the group to flee their tent in low visibility without proper gear. Furthermore, a twenty-twenty-one scientific study published by researchers from EPFL and ETH Zurich demonstrated mathematically that a small slab avalanche could indeed occur on that slope, generating unusual injuries without leaving typical avalanche debris.

**[CONCLUSION]** While questions and skepticism still linger among researchers who doubt snow conditions on that specific gradient, modern science offers a plausible natural explanation for a tragedy that haunted the Urals for generations.
